Executive summary (plain language)

What this project is. A tabletop robot arm was taught, from human demonstrations alone, to pick up a red block and place it in a bowl. The research question is about the harness: a supervisor program wrapped around the learned controller that positions the arm near the block, lets the controller try, checks with its own eyes whether the grasp actually worked, and retries after failures — including grasping by itself if the learned controller keeps missing. Does wrapping an imperfect learned controller this way make the whole system more reliable?

What was done. Two complete experiment rounds in four weeks. Round one failed outright (0/10 on novel block positions) — and instead of stopping there, the failure was traced to its physical causes: a camera mount that crept between sessions, a training set whose coverage had the wrong shape, and a subtle bug where the robot had learned that a person entering the camera frame means the task is over. Round two rebuilt everything those findings implicated — a rigid drift-gated camera, a 201-episode corpus recorded under an audited hygiene protocol, retrained policy, recalibrated artifacts — and re-ran the comparison against fifteen pre-registered targets with the scoring rules fixed in advance.

The verdictAnswer
Does the harness improve success? No — parity. The bare learned controller scored 12/15; the harness scored 12/15 on the same targets. Every harness pass was the controller succeeding on its own first attempt, and the harness's own recovery grasp converted 0 of its 16 live attempts. A formally-met improvement gate on one tier (+20pp) is fully explained by ordinary trial-to-trial variance, not by any harness mechanism.
Did the harness work as designed? The watching half yes, the acting half no. Every component that observes and decides worked flawlessly across all runs: zero false grasp detections, every real failure caught and retried, camera geometry verified before every trial, no safety aborts, and the fail-closed rule never let the robot close its gripper blind. The one component that must move the arm — the scripted recovery grasp — never converted, despite three measured diagnose-and-fix cycles that each removed a real, named defect.
Most important finding Data and instrumentation discipline beat the supervisor layer. The same learning architecture went from 0/10 to 7/10 on novel positions purely from fixing the camera, the corpus, and the accidental visual cue. Once the controller got good, a verify-and-retry wrapper had almost nothing left to add on a task this short — and where it did (three failed trials), its recovery actuation was not yet up to the final-inches control problem.

1  Why a second experiment

v1 ended with a frozen ACT policy that scored 0/10 on held-out block positions and a harness that could not rescue it (0/11) — a negative result with a fully documented causal chain. Before rerunning, the owner raised two concerns and both were measured rather than assumed:

ConcernMeasurementVerdict
Bird's-eye camera shakes during operation and drifts out of gate ArUco-tracked: <1 px motion within episodes (no shake), but up to ~10 px creep between same-evening sessions — past the 3 px gate bound and invisible to a start-of-session-only check Real problem, wrong mechanism: mount creep, not vibration. Fixed by a rigid mount + per-trial drift gate
160-episode corpus too small v1 coverage audit: the held-out zone failure was coverage shape, not just count; corpus also carried the operator-in-frame end-of-episode cue (isolated by occlusion probes) and mid-episode camera geometry changes Rerun with 200-episode target, ring coverage around the held-out zone, and a hygiene protocol

A third, subtler defect drove the protocol design: v1 episodes ended with the operator's hand entering the frame to reset the scene, teaching the policy that person-in-frame means the episode is over (proven by occlusion probes in v1: masking a 107×120 px patch containing the operator's arm changed the policy's behaviour). v2's tail-hygiene rule — every episode ends with the arm parked and the operator fully out of frame, enforced by audit — removed the cue at the source.

2  Experimental design

2.1  Instrumentation and drift control

The agentview camera was moved to an owner-built rigid mount, then commissioned the hard way: the mount settles for minutes after any adjustment (the view slid ~40 px before freezing at 0.1 px/min), so references were recorded only after a measured settle check. Calibration 7709c9ef4715; reference round-trip error 0.05 px. Three drift gates guard every downstream artifact: session-start, session-end (added in v2 — a session recorded across a geometry change is quarantined whole), and per-trial (b601/planner/trial_gate.py — the v1 measurement showed ~10 px creep between same-evening sessions, so the harness runner refuses any trial on a drifted mount).

2.2  Training corpus (four sessions, 205 recorded, 201 kept)

Sessions of 50/50/50/55 teleop episodes were recorded against a placement map that rings the future held-out zone with demonstrations without entering it. Every session passed a same-day audit: frame/row alignment, gripper-trace anchor extraction, tail hygiene (arm parked, operator out of frame at end), and block visibility. Four episodes were quarantined (75, 85, 160, 162), leaving 201. One physical defect was caught and fixed mid-collection: a fixed glare patch over the held-out ring desaturated the block below the detector gate in 11 session-2 starts; the owner re-aimed the lamps (worst-spot saturation S 72→121–136) and the detector gate was recalibrated to S≥55 with the fix verified on re-shot frames.

2.3  Two-tier held-out evaluation (pre-registered)

v1 could not separate “the harness doesn't help” from “nothing multiplies zero”: its only held-out tier was hard extrapolation, where the primitive's per-attempt grasp probability was ~0. v2 evaluates fifteen fixed targets in three tiers, interleaved in one session per condition:

2.4  Choice of frozen primitive: ACT over SmolVLA

Both experiments use ACT as the frozen primitive rather than a pretrained vision-language-action model such as SmolVLA, and v2 deliberately kept that choice. The reasons are experimental, not aesthetic:

ConsiderationACT (chosen)SmolVLA (rejected for this experiment)
Attributability — the core requirement Trained from scratch on this corpus only: every behaviour is causally traceable to the demonstrations. v1's decisive diagnostics (occlusion probes proving the operator-in-frame cue, coverage audits explaining the held-out failure) only work because the corpus is the policy's entire world A pretrained backbone imports behaviour from data we cannot audit; a v1-style post-mortem could no longer separate “our corpus taught this” from “pretraining brought this”
Fit to the harness thesis The thesis needs an imperfect frozen primitive whose competence boundary is known and manipulable — the corpus coverage shape IS that boundary, by construction (ring coverage, held-out zone) Generalization from pretraining would blur the designed competence boundary; the two-tier evaluation loses its meaning if the primitive may have seen similar scenes elsewhere
Task profile Single task, fixed instruction — language conditioning adds nothing here Language conditioning is SmolVLA's main advantage; unused in this design
Compute and iteration ~50M parameters, trains from scratch overnight on the local GPU, 30 Hz chunked inference with headroom; v2 retrained twice without schedule impact Heavier fine-tune and higher inference latency on the same hardware for no benefit the design can use
v1 comparability Holding the architecture constant makes the v1→v2 delta (0/10 → 7/10 held-out) attributable purely to data and instrumentation quality — the report's central causal claim Changing the primitive between rounds would confound exactly that claim

The trade-off is acknowledged: a pretrained VLA might well score higher in absolute terms, and §7 lists it as future work — but as a different experiment, one about primitive strength rather than about what a harness adds around a known-imperfect primitive.

2.5  Policy and rebuilt harness artifacts

ACT was retrained from scratch on the v2 corpus (100k steps, final loss 0.061, near-identical convergence to v1 — checkpoint artifacts/act_b601_v2_20260828). Because the v1 post-mortem showed camera-geometry-dependent artifacts are not transferable, every harness artifact was rebuilt from the v2 corpus and validated against pixels, not self-referential statistics: staging tables (positional leave-one-out median 3.5–5.2°, all ten H/I targets answerable at 28–65 px vs v1's 44–91 px), the grasp verifier (178/182 holds detected, 0/202 false holds on hard negatives, verify only at the lifted pose), the wrist-servo target (corpus median (387,395)), and the state–action map.

4  The harness under test

The harness wraps the frozen primitive in a stage → invoke → verify → re-ground → retry loop: it stages the arm from the block's detected pixel via the staging table plus a wrist-image servo, gives the policy a 40 s attempt, verifies the grasp at a lifted pose (burst majority 3/5), and on a verified failure re-detects the block and retries — from attempt 2 with a scripted recovery grasp (re-servo requiring convergence within 15 px, measured descend, close to the demonstrated hold). Fail-closed everywhere: no servo convergence → no descend, no close. Safety instrumentation (per-tick rate limits, divergence guard, per-trial drift gate, operator-initiated trials) identical to the baseline condition.

The scoring rule was fixed before the counted run: every success is attributed from per-attempt records as policy success (attempt 1) or harness-completed (recovery attempts), and the comparison reports both.

The failure to add successes is localized entirely in the recovery grasp's servo actuation.

6  Recovery grasp post-mortem: two diagnose-and-fix cycles

The recovery grasp converted 0 of 16 live attempts across three outings (six in the counted run, six in shakedown A, four in shakedown B). Each failure was diagnosed from tick-level telemetry, each fix was measured (never tuned), and each cycle removed a real defect — the second cycle restored full actuation authority and still did not convert, which is itself the finding.

7  Conclusions

Did the harness help? No — 12/15 with and without it, every harness pass policy-completed on attempt 1, recovery 0/16.

Did it work as expected? Split it by component, because the answer differs:

  • The passive layers worked as designed. The grasp verifier produced zero false holds across all runs, every real failure was detected and triggered a retry, the per-trial drift gate kept camera geometry pinned, the divergence guard never had to fire, and the fail-closed rule correctly refused every descend the servo couldn't earn. Nothing dangerous or dishonest ever happened.
  • The active layer — recovery actuation — did not. Closed-loop visual servoing at grasp depth converges from ≤60 px but not from the 130+ px errors that deep staging produces at held-out positions, and each failed attempt displaces the block, making the next attempt harder. That is a narrow, named engineering problem (§7.2 lists the measured fixes), not a refutation of the idea — but as tested, the harness never rescued a single trial.

The deeper conclusion of v2 is arguably more valuable than the harness verdict: the same architecture that scored 0/10 held-out in v1 scored 7/10 held-out after the mount, corpus hygiene, coverage shape, and end-of-episode cue were fixed. The biggest lever on real-robot performance in this project was data and instrumentation discipline, not the planner layer — and once the primitive got good, verify-and-retry had almost nothing left to add on a task this short. So the thesis “a harness makes an imperfect frozen primitive more reliable” was given a fair test in its intended regime and did not clear it here; whether it would clear it with working recovery actuation, longer-horizon tasks, or a different primitive remains open — §7.2 frames exactly that as future work. In detail:

  1. The v2 rebuild vindicated the v1 post-mortem. Same architecture, same model scale, same task: 0/10 held-out in v1 became 7/10 held-out (12/15 overall) after the mount, corpus hygiene, coverage shape, and end-of-episode cue were fixed. The largest lever on real-robot performance in this project was data and instrumentation discipline, not the planner layer.
  2. The harness thesis got its fair test in v2 and did not clear it. With an imperfect-but-competent primitive (the regime the thesis targets), the harness matched but did not beat the bare policy: 12/15 vs 12/15, every harness pass policy-completed, the formal +20pp on the interpolation tier fully explained by attempt-1 flips rather than by any recovery. The verification, re-grounding, and safety layers all worked as designed; the scripted recovery grasp — the only harness component that must actuate — converted 0 of 16 live attempts across three measured iterations.
  3. What blocks recovery is now a narrow, named problem: closed-loop visual servoing at grasp depth converges from ≤60 px but not reliably from the 130+ px errors that deep staging produces at held-out positions, and failed attempts displace the block, making retries harder (§7.2 lists the measured next steps).
  4. Scorekeeping held up. Pre-registered targets, gates, and attribution rules fixed before the counted runs made the formally-met-but-hollow gate visible instead of publishable-but-wrong.

7.1  Challenges

Most of this experiment's cost went into problems that never appear in the headline table, and each one was capable of silently corrupting the result:

ChallengeWhat it cost / how it was contained
Physical drift masquerading as policy error The defining challenge of both rounds. Mount creep (~10 px between same-evening sessions), minutes-long settling after any camera adjustment, and a lamp-glare patch that desaturated the block below the detector gate all produce failures indistinguishable from “the policy is bad.” Contained by ArUco references, three drift gates (session-start, session-end, per-trial), settle checks, and the standing rule that anchors are validated against pixels
Sensor session budget (D405 firmware) The wrist camera tolerates one session per reset; any ad-hoc frame grab silently starved the next consumer (one such grab left a stale 3-landmark calibration that looked like a constant 38.5 px drift). Contained by a strict reset-then-capture discipline in every camera consumer
The corpus learns what you didn't mean to teach v1's policy provably learned “operator in frame = episode over.” Protocol hygiene (out-of-frame rule, parked-arm tails) had to be designed, printed on the placement map, and enforced by audit — not assumed
Irreplaceable data 201 teleop episodes cannot be regenerated identically; every session was audited same-day (4 episodes quarantined) and backed up before training, and the backup target remains on-machine — an accepted, documented residual risk
Closed-loop control at grasp depth The hardest unsolved piece: the image Jacobian is posture-dependent (the lift+elbow direction that works on approach self-cancels at depth), gravity droop turns naive command re-anchoring into a downward ratchet, and the pan axis under-executes small position steps. Two telemetry-driven fix cycles restored actuation authority; final-inches convergence from large errors remains open
Honest scorekeeping under pressure to succeed The formally-met gate (+20pp on I) would have read as a positive result without the pre-registered attribution rule. Locking targets, gates, and attribution before the counted runs is what kept the report's headline honest

7.2  Future improvements

If the experiment reopens, in order of expected value per effort:

  1. Recover from the shallow pose, not the deep one. Servo to convergence at the staging height — where the corpus Jacobian is strongest (elbow +13.3 px/deg vs +10.2 at depth) and the wrist camera sees more of the mat — then descend the measured delta. This attacks the 130+ px starting errors directly.
  2. Re-stage between attempts instead of re-servoing in place. The block's agentview pixel is re-detected after every failed attempt anyway; feeding it back through the staging table would reset each retry to table accuracy (28–65 px) instead of inheriting the previous attempt's displacement (measured growing 128→158→312 px across one trial's attempts).
  3. Gate the descend on a displacement check (agentview pixel moved > threshold since staging → restage first), so a nudged block can never be grasped-at blind.
  4. Depth-aware servo gains. The Jacobian is now measured in two regimes; scheduling gain by pose (or re-fitting online from the first few corrections) would extend the convergence basin beyond the current ≤60 px.
  5. A grasp signal that isn't vision. Gripper current/effort sensing at closure would verify the grasp at the moment it happens, catch edge-slips early, and remove the lift-to-verify round trip that costs each attempt ~8 s.
  6. Move the corpus backup off-machine — the risk log's oldest open item.
  7. A pretrained VLA (e.g. SmolVLA) as the primitive — deliberately out of scope here (§2.4), but the natural follow-on question: does a harness add anything around a primitive whose competence does not come from a corpus you control? That is a different thesis and should be registered as such before any data is collected.
